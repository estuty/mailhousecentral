Two plugins, one email, and a gap between them

Most WordPress sites send email with two plugins from two vendors, each doing half the job.

A newsletter plugin writes it

Signup forms, the subscriber list, broadcasts, welcome emails. Its settings know nothing about how mail leaves the server.

An SMTP plugin sends it

Credentials, the transport, the From address. It has never heard of your list, your design or your unsubscribe page.

The seam is where sites break. The newsletter is fine, the transport was reconfigured, and nobody finds out until a customer says the password reset never arrived.

Mailhouse does both jobs. One set of delivery settings, one log for every message, and one design applied to newsletters and WordPress’s own mail alike.

Delivery that tells you what happened

Replaces WP Mail SMTP. Every email WordPress or another plugin sends goes through your chosen transport, and the result is written down.

  • A backup transportIf the main one fails, the message goes out another way. Newsletters can have a transport of their own.
  • An email logEvery message, its result, and the transport’s own error text when it fails. Searchable, with retention, and resend for anything whose body was kept.
  • Bounces that clean up after themselvesSendGrid, Mailgun, Brevo, Postmark and Resend report back. A hard bounce retires the address, a spam complaint unsubscribes, and soft bounces are counted and forgiven on the next delivery.
  • One design for every emailLogo header, branded footer, social links, dark-mode aware. Colours come from your block theme’s palette in one click.
  • Sender controlForce the From address and name, set a default Reply-To, and send bounces to a mailbox somebody reads.
  • Failure alerts and honest test sendsOne email an hour at most when delivery is failing. The test send reports exactly what the transport said.
  • Credentials kept safeEncrypted at rest, or defined as constants in wp-config.php so they never touch the database.

A newsletter that lives on your site

Replaces Noptin. Your list stays in your database, and broadcasts go out through the same delivery settings as everything else.

  • Your own listDouble opt-in, tags, custom fields, CSV import and export.
  • Forms that hold upA shortcode or a block. They work without JavaScript, with a honeypot, a speed trap and a per-IP rate limit.
  • Broadcasts from the post editorSent from a queue in batches, so a big list can’t time out halfway through.
  • AutomationsConfirmation, welcome, and new post notifications: one per post, or a daily or weekly digest.
  • Unsubscribing done properlyOne-click unsubscribe (RFC 8058) and a preferences page in your brand’s colours.
  • Tracking only if you want itOpen and click tracking is off by default and clearly labelled.

Nothing leaves your site except your mail

No analytics, no upsell notices, no phoning home.

No outside requests

The only network calls go to the mail provider you configured, plus a read of the public releases list to check for updates. No site data goes with it.

Message bodies aren’t kept

Unless you ask for it, password resets and receipts don’t sit in your database. The log records what was sent and how it went.

Tracking is your call

Open and click tracking ships switched off. When it’s on, pixels and links are signed so they can’t be forged.

Your list survives an uninstall

Deleting the plugin keeps your subscribers unless you switch that on under Settings, Advanced, first.

Up and sending in five steps

Download mailhouse-1.6.0.zip from the releases page. Use that file, not the source archive: the source unpacks to a folder WordPress treats as a different plugin.

  1. Upload and activate

    Plugins, Add New, Upload Plugin.

  2. Choose a transport

    Mailhouse, Settings, Delivery. Pick one and fill in its fields.

  3. Send a test

    From Tools. It reports what the transport actually said.

  4. Add a signup form

    Paste [mailhouse_form] into a page, or add the Newsletter form block.

  5. Write your first email

    Mailhouse, Emails. Write one, then send.

Three bugs found this way, and fixed

wp_mail() rejected the sender before it could be corrected on hosts whose name has no dot. A second signup replaced the confirmation key while the throttle declined to send a new one, leaving a dead link in somebody’s inbox. And the tracking pixel declared a Content-Length one byte too long.

Questions

Can it replace both my newsletter plugin and my SMTP plugin?

That’s what it’s for. The Tools screen detects Noptin and WP Mail SMTP and imports the subscribers and the delivery settings. Deactivate the old plugins afterwards: with two plugins reconfiguring PHPMailer, it’s a coin toss which one wins.

Will it send 10,000 emails?

Yes, at the rate you set under Newsletter, Sending: a batch a minute, from a queue that survives a timeout. Whether they arrive is down to the transport and your domain’s SPF, DKIM and DMARC records. No plugin can stand in for those.

How do updates work if it isn’t on wordpress.org?

Mailhouse checks its own releases on GitHub on WordPress’s usual twice-daily schedule. A new version appears on the Plugins and Updates screens with its notes and installs in one click. The check only reads the public releases list; no site data goes with it.

Versions before 1.4.0 had no updater, so getting to 1.4.0 or later is a one-time manual upload. Settings, subscribers and the log are untouched.

Where are my SMTP credentials stored?

Encrypted in the options table using your site’s own salts. For anything shared or backed up, define MAILHOUSE_SMTP_PASS (or the matching constant for your provider) in wp-config.php and leave the field empty.

Does it work with other plugins’ email?

Yes. Delivery works by substituting a PHPMailer subclass, so everything WordPress and other plugins do to a message still happens: phpmailer_init, wp_mail_succeeded and wp_mail_failed all fire as normal.

What does it need to run?

WordPress 6.0 or later, PHP 7.4 or later (tested on 8.3), and MySQL 5.7+ or MariaDB 10.3+. There’s no build step and no Composer: five database tables and one option.
